# Endurance Nutrition Guidelines — Reference Summary

Source: Potgieter S. "Sport nutrition: A review of the latest guidelines for exercise and sport
nutrition." *S Afr J Clin Nutr.* 2013;26(1):6-16. A peer-reviewed synthesis of three consensus
bodies — the **ACSM** (American College of Sports Medicine), the **IOC** (International Olympic
Committee, 2010 consensus), and the **ISSN** (International Society for Sports Nutrition).

This summary keeps only what's relevant to **endurance training for a half marathon** — the
source paper also covers strength/power sports (sprinting, weightlifting), which is left out here.
Everything is expressed as **grams per kilogram of body weight (g/kg BW)**, the standard the field
has moved to instead of percentages, specifically because it scales with an individual's own size
and training load rather than a fixed number.

## Energy: fuel the training load, don't fix a number

- The core principle across all three bodies: energy intake should match energy expended in
  training, not sit at a fixed calorie target. Guideline ranges by activity level (ISSN):
  - General activity (30–40 min, 3×/week): **25–35 kcal/kg/day**
  - Moderate-to-high intensity training (2–6 hrs/day, 5–6×/week): **50–80 kcal/kg/day**
- **Estimated Energy Availability (estEA)** = dietary energy intake − exercise energy expended,
  per kg of fat-free mass/day. The IOC's target is **30–45 kcal/kg FFM/day**. Below roughly
  30 kcal/kg FFM/day is linked in the literature to menstrual disruption and reduced bone density
  in female athletes — this is flagged in the source as a real, well-evidenced risk of
  *under*-fuelling relative to training load, not just a performance number.
- Practical takeaway from the review: eat on a schedule (4–6 meals/day of nutrient-dense food)
  rather than waiting for hunger cues, since training itself can suppress appetite even when the
  body's actual energy needs are elevated.

## Carbohydrate (g/kg BW/day)

**Daily, scaled to training volume** (the ranges that matter for base → build → peak):
| Training load | g/kg BW/day |
|---|---|
| General/base-phase activity | 3–5 |
| Moderate-to-high intensity, 2–3 hrs/day | 5–8 |
| Endurance programme, moderate-high intensity, 1–3 hrs/day | 6–10 |

**Before a long run or race** (IOC): **1–4 g/kg BW, 1–4 hours before** — more lead time allows a
bigger meal further out; a tight window (e.g. an early race start) calls for a smaller amount
closer in.

**Carb-loading before the half** (events >60–90 min): current evidence-based guidance is
**7–12 g/kg BW/day for the 24 hours before**, or **10–12 g/kg BW/day for 36–48 hours before** —
notably *not* the older "three days of loading," which the review notes was based on research in
less-trained individuals.

**During the run** (>60 min): **30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour**, all three bodies agree on this.
As a 6–8% carbohydrate solution; combining glucose + fructose sources increases uptake versus
glucose alone. Runs under 45 minutes don't need this at all; a true half-marathon finish time
likely sits right at the edge of where this starts to matter.

**After the run**: **1.0–1.5 g/kg BW within 30 minutes**, repeated every 2 hours for 4–6 hours if
turning around for another session within 8 hours (less critical with a full rest day between).

## Protein (g/kg BW/day)

**Daily**:
| Training load | g/kg BW/day |
|---|---|
| General fitness | 0.8–1.0 |
| Moderate intense training | 1.0–1.5 |
| High-volume intense training | 1.5–2.0 |

All three bodies converge on roughly **1.2–2.0 g/kg BW/day** as the athlete range.

**Post-exercise** (the single most consistent recommendation across all three bodies):
**~20g of high-quality protein within 30 minutes**, alongside carbohydrate — a **carb:protein
ratio of roughly 3–4:1** is the commonly cited recovery target. Quality matters: milk/whey protein
(high leucine content) outperforms isolated soy for muscle protein synthesis in the cited studies.

## Fat

- **20–35% of total energy intake** (ACSM) — don't drop below 20%, since fat carries fat-soluble
  vitamin absorption and essential fatty acids.
- High-fat diets (>30%) aren't recommended for athletes — displaces the carbohydrate needed for
  training and racing.

## Fluid & electrolytes

- Keep body weight loss during exercise **under 2%** — both ISSN and IOC use this threshold for
  where performance starts to decline.
- **During exercise**: 0.5–2 L/hour, in small amounts (150–200ml) every 15–20 minutes. Add sodium
  for exercise lasting over 2 hours or in hot/humid conditions.
- **After exercise**: roughly **1.5 L of fluid per kg of body weight lost**, with sodium included
  to aid retention and restore thirst properly.
- Don't rely on thirst alone as a gauge — weighing before/after a long run is the way the source
  recommends calibrating an individual sweat rate.

## Micronutrients — worth knowing given the training context

- No blanket supplementation is recommended if energy needs are met from a varied diet.
- **Vitamin D**: the review flags indoor training, higher latitudes, and limited sun exposure as
  risk factors for deficiency — worth having checked if there's any doubt, rather than
  supplementing routinely without reason.
- **Iron**: specifically flagged for endurance athletes and female athletes, since endurance
  training increases iron turnover.
- **Calcium**: tied to the same bone-density risk noted above under low energy availability.
- **Antioxidants**: current guidance is *against* routine high-dose supplementation — get these
  from food (fruit, veg, whole grains) rather than pills; there's no strong evidence they help, and
  some evidence high doses can blunt training adaptations.

## Supplements — evidence tier (ISSN classification)

- **Apparently effective, generally safe**: creatine, protein/EAAs, caffeine, carbohydrate-
  electrolyte solutions, sodium bicarbonate, beta-alanine.
- **Possibly effective**: branched-chain amino acids, calcium, conjugated linoleic acid.
- IOC's strongly-evidenced ergogenic list for endurance specifically: **caffeine** (endurance +
  reaction time) and **nitrate** (aerobic endurance) are the two most relevant to a half marathon.
- Caution flagged throughout the source: supplement regulation is weak, contamination and
  mislabelling are real risks, and — the review is explicit about this — no amount of
  supplementation compensates for inadequate daily diet. Food first.

## What this means for the actual training block

- **Base phase** (0–5km easy runs): the general-activity carbohydrate range (3–5 g/kg BW/day) is
  plenty. This phase doesn't need carb-loading or during-run fuelling at all.
- **Build phase** (long runs 8–14km): moves into the 5–8 g/kg BW/day carbohydrate range. Once a
  long run crosses ~60 minutes, 30–60g of carbs/hour during it and refuelling within 30 minutes
  after both start to matter.
- **Peak week** (17km long run): genuine endurance-nutrition territory. Worth treating this run as
  a rehearsal for race-day fuelling — practice the pre-run meal (1–4g/kg BW carbs, 1–4 hours
  before), during-run carbs, and the 30-minute post-run recovery window, so race day isn't the
  first time trying any of it.
- **Taper/race week**: this is when the 24–48 hour carb-loading window actually applies
  (7–12 g/kg BW/day for 24h, or 10–12 g/kg for 36–48h before race day) — moderate and
  evidence-based, not a multi-day mega-load.
- Day to day, the two numbers most likely to matter without needing to track anything obsessively:
  **protein in the 1.2–1.5 g/kg BW/day range**, and the **~20g protein + carbs within 30 minutes
  after a session** recovery habit.
